# Research Notes — Spatiotemporal GNN Benchmarking for Subnational Influenza Forecasting

## Project Overview

This project investigates whether graph neural networks (GNNs) can improve subnational epidemic forecasting by incorporating spatial connectivity between administrative regions. The primary task is forecasting weekly influenza incidence rates at German Kreis level (NUTS3, ~400 regions) at a horizon of three weeks ahead. A secondary planned comparison involves daily COVID-19 case counts in the Netherlands at gemeente level (LAU) and Germany at Kreis level, where mobility-driven spatial spread is hypothesised to be stronger.

The codebase is a self-contained Python pipeline built around an `EpiConfig` dataclass that parameterises the full experiment — country, disease, spatial resolution, temporal frequency, forecast horizon, features, and normalisation. Data flows through an `EpiDataOrchestrator` into typed dataloaders (`DeepDataLoaderManager`, `GraphDataLoaderManager`) and then into model classes built on a common `DeepModel` base. Models implement a strategy pattern that decouples the training loop from the forward pass, allowing GNN, LSTM, and baseline models to share infrastructure. Evaluation is handled by an `Evaluator` class that compiles per-node, per-model predictions and metrics, with a `PeakEvaluator` extension for performance specifically around epidemic peaks. The codebase is well-structured with clear separation of concerns, though some technical debt remains in the `DeepModel` god class and incomplete validator logic.

## Models and Graphs Benchmarked

Six GNN architectures were benchmarked: a spatially-dominant GCN2 (no temporal unit — graph must carry all structured inductive bias), a DecoupledGCN with per-node learned gates that mix self-information and neighbourhood aggregation, a GCN-LSTM, a GAT-LSTM, a GCN-TCN, and a plain LSTM without any graph. Each GNN was run with six graph configurations: an identity graph (self-loops only), a geographic contiguity graph, three commuter-flow variants derived from 2021 Pendlerstatistik data at varying sparsity thresholds, and a random graph. All graphs are static. Normalisation is applied globally across nodes. Baselines include persistence, climatology, and ClimaScale. The primary evaluation metric is the concordance correlation coefficient (CCC), which jointly penalises correlation and mean/variance mismatch and is more appropriate than MSE for epidemic curve evaluation.

## Key Experimental Findings

All neural network models substantially and consistently outperform persistence, with mean CCC gains of approximately +0.18 to +0.21 across all 400 nodes. The GCN2 model with geographic neighbourhood graph achieves the highest mean CCC gain (+0.21), and 398–399 out of 400 nodes beat persistence across most model configurations. These are robust, consistent results.

However, the critical finding is that graph structure contributes almost nothing to this gain. The plain LSTM — with no graph at all — achieves a mean CCC delta of +0.208, statistically indistinguishable from the best GNN (+0.211). The identity graph GCN, which performs no neighbourhood aggregation, achieves +0.176, only marginally behind geo neighbours (+0.211). The difference between the best and worst graph type is approximately 0.03 CCC points — smaller than the expected variance across random initialisations, which were not yet systematically measured. Furthermore, when filtering to the hardest 80 nodes by persistence CCC — the nodes where spatial information should matter most — the violin distributions across graph types are visually indistinguishable.

The variance ratio (VR) analysis reveals the mechanism behind the GNN gains: all GNN models show systematic underdispersion (mean VR ≈ 0.83, with 87–95% of nodes underdispersed), meaning they predict flatter epidemic curves than observed. Gains over persistence come entirely from better Pearson correlation (shape tracking) rather than amplitude calibration. This is consistent with MSE training on normalised data encouraging regression to the mean, and it explains why different graph structures produce similar CCC values — they are all learning the same smoothed temporal representation, with the graph providing minimal additional structure.

## Interpretation and Paths Forward

The most likely explanation for the absence of graph signal is that weekly temporal aggregation averages away the spatial diffusion process. By the time weekly incidence is observed at Kreis level, influenza has already propagated to adjacent regions — there is no residual lead-lag structure for the graph to exploit. This is consistent with the literature on influenza spread velocity relative to reporting frequency.

Two paths forward are viable. The first is to frame the current results as a methodological null result: a systematic benchmark showing that graph structure does not improve over a graph-free LSTM for weekly subnational influenza forecasting in Germany, and that identity and random graph baselines — rarely reported in the GNN literature — are necessary to distinguish architectural gains from spatial gains. The second is to pivot the primary analysis to COVID-19 at daily resolution in the Netherlands, where mobility-driven spatial spread was demonstrably faster and commuter graphs may carry genuine predictive signal. The existing pipeline supports this task with a configuration change and a new graph. Comparing the two diseases within the same framework — influenza (spatial signal absent) versus COVID (spatial signal potentially present) — would constitute a stronger and more novel comparative contribution than either disease alone.

Before any further modelling, the priority should be running multiple random seeds on the LSTM and best GNN to quantify within-model variance, which will determine whether the observed between-graph differences are meaningful or fall within noise.
